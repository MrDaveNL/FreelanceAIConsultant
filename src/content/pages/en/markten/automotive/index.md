## AI applications in automotive

Think of AI in automotive and self-driving cars or smart factories come to mind quickly. For a motor retailer or dealer, the value sits closer to home: in the steps between the first enquiry and the next service visit.

The bottlenecks are familiar across most motor retailers:

- **Leads from many channels:** enquiries through your own site, advertising platforms, the manufacturer and the phone, not all followed up equally fast.
- **Used cars:** buying in, pricing and writing listings take time per car, while the margin depends on how quickly a car sells again.
- **The workshop:** scheduling is tight, technicians piece together technical information, and job cards get filled in after the fact.
- **Customer contact:** questions about appointments, repair status or a quote also come in during evenings and weekends.
- **Scattered data:** the dealer management system, the CRM and the manufacturer's own systems don't always talk to each other.

AI can help on each of these points, but not all at once. The question isn't which application is possible, but which delivers the most for your business.

## AI for motor retailers and dealers

A dealer is a retailer, a service business and a link in a manufacturer's chain, all at once. Those three roles decide where AI fits.

**As a retailer**, it's about speed and follow-up. An AI application can merge incoming enquiries, pull out the key details (model, trade-in, financing preference), and give a salesperson a draft first response. The salesperson stays the point of contact; the AI makes sure nothing is left unanswered.

**As a service business**, the gain sits in the workshop: summarising complaints when booking an appointment, preparing a job card, and searching technical documentation. That last one only works if the documentation is digital and current, which isn't always the case.

**As a link in the chain**, you're working within the manufacturer's systems and agreements. Some functionality arrives automatically through the importer. It pays to find out what's already in the pipeline before buying something yourself.

My own focus sits with manufacturing, retail, financial services and professional services, not automotive specifically, but a motor retailer touches all of those worlds. The questions are the same: what do you tackle first, which vendor do you choose, and how do you keep it working. What that looks like in a manufacturing setting is covered in the case on an [AI roadmap for a global manufacturer](/cases/ai-roadmap-global-manufacturer): bringing scattered pilots together into one roadmap, with a clear decision on what stops.

## AI assistants

An AI assistant for motor retailers can face two ways: towards the customer, or towards your own people.

**Customer-facing assistants** answer questions on the website, via WhatsApp or by phone, and can, for example, book a service appointment. They work well for predictable questions, not for complaints or negotiation. Agree in advance when the assistant hands over to a person, and test that with real conversations.

**Internal assistants** help salespeople and technicians: ask a question about a model, a warranty condition or a work instruction, and get an answer with the source attached. Here, the quality of your own documentation is what decides the outcome. An assistant searching messy files gives messy answers.

A step further are AI agents, which carry out tasks in your systems on their own, such as booking an appointment and sending the confirmation. What's realistic there right now is covered in [AI agent examples](/ai-agents/examples).

I don't build or sell assistants myself. I do help you choose between what your DMS vendor, the manufacturer or a specialist provider offers, and test whether the promise holds up. ## AI automation in automotive

AI automation works best on tasks that happen often, have a clear start and end, and currently involve a lot of retyping. In a motor retailer, that typically means:

- transferring data from purchase invoices, registration documents and damage reports into your systems;
- drafting listing copy for used cars from the specifications;
- preparing warranty and goodwill claims so a staff member only needs to check them.

Two caveats. First, the technology is rarely the problem. Where it stalls is when nobody owns the process after the pilot, or the workflow isn't allowed to change. Second, time saved on paper is only a real gain once someone actually spends that time differently.

### Which rules apply?

For most dealer applications, the AI Act is straightforward. A chatbot or a text generator isn't a high-risk application, but a transparency duty has applied since August 2026: people need to know they're dealing with AI. It's different if you use AI to screen job applicants or assess an individual's creditworthiness. Those applications sit on the AI Act's high-risk list. Under the Digital Omnibus, the requirements for them now apply from 2 December 2027, according to the [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai). The Act also reaches UK dealer groups with EU operations or EU customers for their AI output, under the same [Article 2](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2) scope used across this site; the UK has no AI Act of its own and regulates AI through existing sector regulators instead, per the government's [pro-innovation approach](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-regulation-a-pro-innovation-approach).

AI built into the vehicle itself, such as driver-assistance systems, is a manufacturer question, governed through vehicle type approval rather than the AI Act. The UK runs its own GB type approval scheme rather than the EU's, but it directly incorporates the same UN vehicle regulations for cyber security (UN R155) and software updates (UN R156) through GB-specific legislation, phased in from 1 June 2026 for new vehicle types ([Vehicle Certification Agency](https://www.vehicle-certification-agency.gov.uk/blog/new-gb-type-approval-legislation-cyber-security-software-updates-introduced); [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/gb-type-approval-scheme-cyber-security-and-software-update-requirements)). That's primarily a matter for the manufacturer and importer. UK GDPR, of course, continues to apply to all customer and staff data.
