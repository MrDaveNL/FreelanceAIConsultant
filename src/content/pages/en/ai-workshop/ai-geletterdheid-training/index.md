## What is AI literacy?

AI literacy means that people who work with AI systems have the knowledge, skills and understanding to do so responsibly. The European Commission's own guidance stresses that it isn't only about the technical workings, but also the social, ethical and practical side: understanding how to interpret an AI system's output, for example, and what a decision made with AI's help means for the people it affects.

In practice, it comes down to four questions a staff member should be able to answer:

- What does this system do, and where is it unreliable?
- Which data can and can't go into it?
- How do I check the output before I use it?
- Who do I report it to if something goes wrong?

An AI literacy training is one way to answer those questions across your whole organisation. It isn't the only way, and a training on its own isn't enough either.

## What the AI Act asks

Article 4 of the AI Act has applied since 2 February 2025 to providers and deployers of AI systems. You become a deployer quickly: an organisation that uses AI tools in its work falls under it.

The text of Article 4 was amended in 2026. On 19 November 2025, the European Commission proposed, in the Digital Omnibus package, replacing the obligation for organisations with a task for the Commission and member states to promote AI literacy. That proposal wasn't adopted unchanged. The eventual amendment [took effect on 27 July 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-omnibus-enters-force), as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744. The state of play as of October 2026, according to the [European Commission's own explanation](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers):

- **The duty remains in place** for providers and deployers of AI systems.
- **It has become a duty of effort:** organisations take measures that support the development of their staff's and other affected people's AI literacy. There's no longer a specific or "sufficient" level prescribed.
- **Context counts:** people's technical knowledge, experience, education and training, and the context in which the systems are used.
- **The Commission and member states** get a bigger role in promoting AI literacy.
- **Supervision** sits with national market surveillance authorities, who supervise and enforce from 2 August 2026.
- **For UK organisations**, this duty only applies once the AI Act itself applies to them, for example once they place an AI system on the EU market or its output is used there; the UK has no equivalent literacy duty of its own, as the UK government's own approach relies on existing sector regulators rather than a cross-cutting AI law.

The bar has shifted from a result to an effort, but doing nothing doesn't meet it. How Article 4 relates to the rest of the Act is covered on the [EU AI Act for businesses](/ai-policy/ai-act) page.

*This isn't legal advice. Interpretation of the law is still developing; have its application to your organisation reviewed by a lawyer or your compliance function.*

## What the training covers

There's no prescribed curriculum, and that's just as well. The European Commission describes it as an approach with no "one size fits all": what's needed depends on the risks, the roles and the context. That's why I tailor the content to your organisation. An AI literacy training might look like this:

1. **How AI works:** what generative AI does, why it makes mistakes that sound convincing, and what that means for oversight.
2. **Your own systems:** which AI tools are in use across the organisation, what they're meant for, and what they're not.
3. **Risks and rules:** personal data and GDPR, confidential information, bias, and the outline of the AI Act.
4. **Agreements:** what your internal rules are, who's the point of contact, and how to report an incident.
5. **Practice:** recognisable tasks from your own work, including checking an outcome.

For teams that then genuinely start using the tools, a deeper dive follows. That can take the shape of a practical [Copilot training](/ai-workshop/copilot-training) for organisations with Microsoft 365, or a hands-on [ChatGPT training](/ai-workshop/chatgpt-training). An overview of all formats is on the [AI workshops and training](/ai-workshop) page.

If internal agreements don't exist yet, it's worth drafting them at the same time. A training about rules that don't exist stays vague. The [AI policy](/ai-policy) page covers what belongs in one.

## Demonstrating that you comply

According to the European Commission, no certificate is required. You can keep an internal record of the training and other initiatives you've run. What matters is being able to show which measures you've taken and why they fit your situation.

A useful four-step structure, which I use as a basis:

<figure class="diagram" role="img" aria-label="Four steps to build and record AI literacy: identify, set the goal, carry out, and evaluate, after which the cycle starts again">
<ol class="flow">
<li><b>Identify</b>Which AI systems do we use, who works with them, and what are the risks?</li>
<li><b>Set the goal</b>What does each role need to know, depending on risk and context?</li>
<li><b>Carry out</b>Training, agreements, a point of contact and repetition.</li>
<li><b>Evaluate</b>Is it working, and what's changed about the tools or the risks?</li>
</ol>
<figcaption>An ongoing cycle, built on the four steps common in regulatory guidance on AI literacy.</figcaption>
</figure>

What you record at a minimum:

- an overview of the AI systems in use and who works with them;
- per role, what someone needs to know and which measure matches it;
- who has taken which training or instruction, and when;
- the internal agreements on AI use and where staff can find them;
- a point in time when you review the whole thing again.

Agree beforehand, as part of a training, that you receive the content, the participant list and the agreements made in a form you can add straight to that file. Then the record-keeping isn't a separate job afterwards.

<p class="scenario-label">Example scenario</p>
<p>A logistics company with 260 staff uses an AI assistant in the office, a planning module with forecasts, and a tool that reads freight documents. A screening tool for recruitment is ready to go live. The consultant works through the four steps with HR and the data protection officer: 140 people turn out to work with at least one system, across five roles.</p>
<p>Everyone takes a ninety-minute foundation session, run across six groups. Twelve planners also get a half-day deeper dive on checking forecasts. The screening tool doesn't go live for now, because AI in recruitment can fall under the high-risk rules and is reviewed separately first.</p>
<p>After three months, there's a file with the overview of systems, the measure per role, the internal agreements and the attendance lists: 132 of the 140 people completed the foundation session. A review follows in six months.</p>
