## Why AI matters now for small businesses

AI for small business has moved from experiment to everyday tool over the past few years. You no longer need a data team or your own model to work with it: an AI assistant, or the AI features in your accounting, CRM or office software, is often enough to win the first bit of time back.

At the same time, adoption among smaller businesses still lags behind larger ones. UK government research found that 16% of businesses overall were using AI, but only 14% of micro businesses (5 to 9 employees), against 23% of mid-sized businesses and 36% of large ones ([DSIT, AI Adoption Research](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research), fieldwork February to May 2025). Less in-house expertise, tighter budgets, and no dedicated team to drive it explain most of that gap. From what I see in practice, the businesses that hold back usually aren't facing a technical barrier. It's more often a lack of familiarity with what's possible, plus unresolved questions about privacy and the legal side of things.

A small business does have one advantage larger organisations lack: short lines of communication. The owner who makes the call usually knows the work first-hand. There's no steering group needed to let one team try a new way of working. Starting with a clear focus means finding out faster whether something works.

The flip side is real too. Lower volume means an application pays for itself more slowly. And without an IT department, everything lands on a handful of people doing it alongside their day job. That's exactly why choosing well matters more here than in a large company. The broader picture of what AI means for organisations is covered on the [AI for business](/ai-for-business) overview page.

## Examples of AI applications in small businesses

The applications that work best in a small business have a few things in common: the task comes up often, has roughly the same shape each time, and someone already checks the result before it goes out the door. A few examples by area.

### Customer service

How do you use AI for customer service in a small business? Don't start with a chatbot that handles customers on its own. Start behind the scenes:

- automatically sorting incoming emails by topic and urgency;
- having draft replies suggested based on earlier answers and your terms, which a staff member checks and sends;
- summarising long customer histories before someone calls back.

That way a person stays in control, and you learn which questions are suitable for further automation. Only once that's running well does a customer-facing assistant for simple questions become a logical next step.

### Sales and quotes

Quotes and proposals are manual work in many small businesses that's nearly the same every time. AI can draft a first version based on earlier quotes, the customer's request and your price list. Summarising a sales call and preparing for a meeting with information from your CRM also saves time. You always check the price and any commitments yourself.

### Marketing

Marketing is many owners' first encounter with AI: website copy, newsletters and social media. It pays off most on variants and rewrites, such as turning one article into three posts, or adapting a piece for a different audience. Strategy and your own tone of voice still need your own judgement; without steering, everything starts to sound the same.

### Admin

Reading invoices, receipts and delivery notes, entering data into your books, flagging anomalies: this is dull, frequent work with a clear check step, which makes it one of the best starting points. Many accounting packages already have their own features for this. Check there first before buying something separate.

### Internal knowledge

In a small business, a lot of knowledge sits in a handful of people's heads and in folders nobody searches any more. An AI assistant that answers based on your own manuals, procedures and past projects helps new staff get up to speed faster, and makes you less dependent on the one colleague who knows everything. The condition: the documents need to be current. An assistant trained on outdated procedures gives outdated answers.

## Risks and threats

AI brings different risks for a small business than for a large organisation. Fewer rules and fewer specialists mean mistakes go unnoticed for longer. The main threats:

- **Customer data in the wrong tool.** A staff member who pastes a customer record into a free AI tool may be sharing personal data with a party you have no agreement with. That's a data protection risk and a trust risk.
- **Relying on one person.** Often there's one enthusiastic staff member who set everything up. If they leave, nobody else knows how it works.
- **Blind trust.** AI tools write persuasively, even when the content is wrong. A quote with a made-up discount, or an answer with the wrong terms, reaches the customer directly in a small business.
- **Costs that creep up.** Separate subscriptions per person and per tool add up, without anyone keeping track of what they deliver.
- **Vendor claims.** Plenty of software is marketed as "AI-powered" these days. Always ask what it actually does, with which data, and what happens if you want to switch.
- **Regulation.** The AI Act also applies to small businesses, though the obligations depend on how you use AI, and its reach isn't limited to EU-based businesses: under [Article 2 of the Act](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2), it also applies to a UK small business that places an AI system on the EU market, or whose AI system's output is used in the EU. For most small-business applications, such as text and summaries, the requirements are limited. Anyone using AI in decisions about people, such as recruitment, needs to pay closer attention. The UK itself has no AI Act and regulates AI through existing sector regulators instead, as set out in the UK government's [AI regulation: a pro-innovation approach](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-regulation-a-pro-innovation-approach), so a UK-only small business with no EU market presence and no EU users of its AI output sits outside the Act's scope.

None of these risks is a reason not to start. They are a reason to agree a few ground rules before everyone finds their own way of working.

## First steps

Start with AI in your small business with as little upfront work as possible, but with a few deliberate choices:

<figure class="diagram" role="img" aria-label="Five first steps with AI for a small business: choose a task, set a baseline, agree ground rules, test for four weeks, then decide">
<ol class="flow">
<li><b>Choose one task</b>Frequent, time-consuming and already checked, such as quotes or sorting email.</li>
<li><b>Measure it now</b>How much time does the task cost per week, and how often does something go wrong?</li>
<li><b>Set ground rules</b>Which tool, which data can't go in, and who checks the result.</li>
<li><b>Test for four weeks</b>With two or three colleagues, in the real work, with real customer queries.</li>
<li><b>Decide</b>Carry on, adjust or stop, based on the measurement, not the feeling.</li>
</ol>
<figcaption>A first AI application in a small business: start small, measure, and only then expand.</figcaption>
</figure>

A few tips to go with it:

1. **Look at what you already have first.** Plenty of office, accounting and CRM software has AI features you're already paying for but not using.
2. **Choose a business licence.** That gives you agreements on what happens to your data. Free accounts are no place for customer data.
3. **Make the work visible.** Let the colleagues using it share their approach. One good way of working that the whole team uses delivers more than ten separate experiments.
4. **Keep the sums honest.** Count your own people's time, not just the licence.
