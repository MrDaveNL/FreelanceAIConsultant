## What does AI implementation involve?

AI implementation is the process that takes an AI application from idea or pilot to daily use. That sounds like a technical job, but the technical part is often the smallest piece. The models themselves are good and widely available. What makes an implementation succeed or fail is whether the application fits the work, whether the data can support it, and whether someone stays responsible for it after go-live.

An implementation therefore covers more than building:

- **A sharp use case** with a measurable goal and an owner in the business.
- **Data and systems** that feed the application, including the integrations.
- **A choice between building, buying or a partner**, followed by a delivery partner who delivers what was agreed.
- **Adoption**: people changing how they work, not just being handed a licence.
- **Management, measurement and governance**, so the application still does its job a year from now.

It matters little whether this is an internal assistant, document processing, a customer-service chatbot or a predictive model. The building blocks stay the same; only the emphasis shifts. Where the focus is mainly on automating repetitive steps in a process, you'll find the specific points to watch under AI automation.

## From AI strategy to practical implementation

Between an AI strategy and a working application sits a gap where many organisations get stuck. The strategy names opportunities and priorities. The implementation has to turn that into concrete choices: which process, which data, which vendor, which budget, which owner, and at what point you stop if it isn't working.

A good [AI roadmap](/ai-consulting/ai-strategy) does half of that translation already: it sequences use cases and ties them to owners and decision points. If there's no roadmap yet, that's not a reason to wait. It is a reason to keep the first project small and well defined, so you learn what your organisation can handle before starting the second and third application.

### Use case and business case

Every implementation starts with the question of what you genuinely want to improve. "We want a chatbot" is a solution; "first-line customer queries take too long to resolve" is a problem you can work with. Together we define the goal in measurable terms, name an owner in the business, and work through the business case, including the running costs after go-live. We also agree in advance at what outcome we stop.

**Delivered:** a scoped use case with a goal, owner, baseline measurement, success threshold and stop criteria.

### Data and systems

Most implementations get stuck on data, not on the model. I map out which sources the application needs, how reliable they are, which integrations are required and who manages the data. Where personal data is involved, the question of whether a data protection impact assessment is needed belongs at the start, not just before go-live.

**Delivered:** a data picture that flags the gaps to close first, plus a privacy and risk assessment.

### Building and testing

The build is done by your own team, or by a party you contract directly. I charge no margin on partners and hold no commissions or partner status with vendors. My work during this phase:

- building the case for building, buying or using a partner, piece by piece;
- for an external party: a selection against criteria agreed in advance, tested on your own data (that's essentially a [Vendor Review](/services/vendor-review));
- setting the acceptance criteria and test set the delivery will be judged against;
- holding the steering group's attention on scope, timeline and quality, and raising it with the delivery partner where needed;
- making sure ownership, management and exit terms are written into the contract.

The pilot runs in the real workflow, with real data, not in a separate test environment alongside the work.

**Delivered:** a reasoned build choice, a test set with acceptance criteria, and a delivery checked against them.

### Adoption and training

A working application nobody uses delivers nothing. Adoption is something you design: with concrete scenarios per role rather than a blank input field, with champions inside the teams themselves, and with clear rules about what is and isn't allowed. Where needed, I run workshops and training myself for the teams who will be using it.

**Delivered:** an adoption approach per role, with scenarios, champions and working agreements.

### Monitoring and optimising

AI applications sometimes degrade quietly as reality shifts. That's why a measurement set-up belongs in from the start: what you measure, how often, who looks at it, and what happens if quality drops. Alongside that, we set up light governance: a register of applications, a gate before anything goes live, a periodic check and an escalation path.

It's also worth checking whether the application would count as high-risk under the EU AI Act. The Act's scope isn't limited to EU-based organisations: under [Article 2 of the Act](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2), it also applies to UK organisations that place an AI system on the EU market, or whose AI system's output is used in the EU, regardless of where the organisation is established. For most high-risk systems these obligations are being phased in from 2 December 2027, and from 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in products, following the EU's Digital Omnibus package. The UK itself has no AI Act: it regulates AI through existing sector regulators instead, applying shared principles rather than one cross-cutting AI law, as set out in the UK government's [AI regulation: a pro-innovation approach](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-regulation-a-pro-innovation-approach). So the relevant obligations depend on your sector, and on whether your organisation places AI systems on the EU market or has EU users of their output.

## AI implementation for SMEs

In smaller businesses the principles are the same, but the circumstances differ. There's less budget for experiments, often no dedicated data team, and the owner-manager is client, budget holder and sometimes user all at once. That's not purely a disadvantage: decisions move faster and the distance between an idea and the shop floor is short. Implementing AI in an SME is mostly about focus: one application that lands well is worth more than five half-finished ones.

### Where do you start without a big IT team?

Start with the work, not the technology. Ask every team which task from last week cost more time than it was worth. That produces a usable list within an hour per team. From it, choose an application that:

- runs on a single data source and doesn't need heavy integrations;
- can be filled by an existing product or platform, so you don't have to build;
- has a clear owner who directly benefits from it;
- can show measurable results within a few months.

Buying is often the cheapest way for a small or medium-sized business to learn what it actually needs. You can always build later, with a sharper specification. What you do need is someone who checks the choices independently, especially when a vendor puts an attractive demo and a multi-year contract on the table.

## Common mistakes in AI implementation

Most mistakes aren't technical. They're about ownership, management and change. These are the patterns that keep coming back:

- **The pilot answered the wrong question.** "Can it work technically?" is almost always yes. The real question is whether it delivers value at the volume, and in the process, it needs to run in.
- **The pilot ran outside the real work.** A separate environment with enthusiastic testers says little about scaling up.
- **Nobody owns the next phase.** The project lead is done at delivery, and the business has no capacity or budget set aside.
- **Management wasn't in the business case.** Monitoring, model updates and an up-to-date test set cost time on an ongoing basis.
- **The organisation wasn't allowed to change.** The saving only exists if the work is genuinely reorganised around it.

Many of these mistakes can be avoided with good preparation.

## Results and case studies

Two case studies show what implementation guidance looks like in practice. At a financial services firm, the work was [AI-assisted document analysis in the KYC process](/cases/kyc-document-analysis-financial-services). There, the hardest part was designing the workflow itself: which steps are extraction and which are judgement, and how you keep every decision traceable to a person. My role was in the process analysis, the human-in-the-loop design, alignment with compliance, and guiding the vendor and the implementation team.

At a professional services firm, the work was an [internal AI assistant for knowledge workers](/cases/internal-ai-assistant-knowledge-workers). The tool itself worked fine; the problem was adoption. The fix was a handful of concrete scenarios instead of open-ended chat, internal champions, and a deliberate choice not to build certain features at all.

<p>A technical wholesaler with 120 staff no longer wants to retype incoming order confirmations from suppliers into its ERP system. Three back-office staff spend around 30 hours a week on this between them: roughly 900 documents at two minutes each.</p>
<p>The adviser works with the procurement manager to set acceptance criteria and a test set of 200 real documents, runs two document-processing vendors against that test set, and keeps scope and timeline on track in the steering group. <p>After eight weeks, 78 percent of documents go through without correction, and manual work drops from 30 to around 11 hours a week. Deliberately not automated: the non-standard formats used by two large suppliers, since that integration would cost more than it would save.</p>
