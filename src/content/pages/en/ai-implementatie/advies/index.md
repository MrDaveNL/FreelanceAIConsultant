## Why upfront advice pays off

An AI project gets expensive the moment you discover an earlier choice was wrong. A use case with no owner, a vendor contract with no exit clause, or a dataset that's messier than expected: during the build, each of those discoveries costs weeks and budget. Catch the same thing upfront, and it costs a few conversations.

AI implementation consulting isn't a luxury reserved for large organisations, then. It's the cheapest phase of the whole project, and the one where you have the most influence over the outcome. For project leads and IT, it means a clear brief for the delivery partner. For leadership, it means a decision that's backed by evidence rather than resting on a convincing demo alone.

This consulting is one part of the broader [guidance through AI implementation](/ai-implementation), from use case to management. Looking for advice on AI in a broader sense, such as strategy or prioritisation across the whole organisation? See [AI consulting](/ai-consulting).

## What I advise on

Four topics decide whether an implementation succeeds. They belong on the table together, because they affect each other: a different use case calls for different data, and different data can flip the build-or-buy choice.

### Choosing the use case

The first question isn't "what can AI do?" but "which problem is costing us too much time, money or quality right now?". I help assess candidate applications the same way: on value, feasibility, data readiness, change load, and whether there's an owner in the business who genuinely benefits. The result is a short list, with a reasoned list of what didn't make the cut. For the kinds of applications that typically deliver results, see [which AI use cases deliver results](/insights/which-ai-use-cases-deliver-results).

### Build versus buy

Building it yourself, buying a product, or working with a partner: that choice shapes your costs, your dependency and your agility for years. Build only what sets you apart, buy what's a commodity, and choose a partner when you want to steer the outcome without owning the technology. Factor in the running costs over several years, and agree upfront how you get out if it doesn't work. The full decision framework is in [build, buy or partner](/insights/build-buy-or-partner). Choose a partner, and you contract them directly; how I work with that is covered on the [partner network](/partner-network) page.

### Data and privacy

I map out what data the application needs, where it comes from, how reliable it is, and who's responsible for it. Alongside that, I look at the privacy side. If the application processes personal data with a significant likelihood of high privacy risk, a data protection impact assessment is required under UK GDPR, [as set out by the ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/guides-to-the-uk-gdpr/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments/). That question belongs at the start, not just before go-live. I also look at what data you're allowed to send to an external service, and what agreements with the vendor that requires.

### Change management

Every AI application that saves time changes someone's job. That conversation often gets postponed until after delivery, and then the business case only holds up if something changes that nobody's bought into. I help name upfront what needs to change, who decides on it, and how you bring along the people who'll actually use it, with concrete scenarios per role rather than one blanket rollout.

An engagement starts with a thirty-minute conversation. You explain where things stand, I ask questions, and I say at the end what I'd do, even if that means you don't need me.

After that, an intake scopes the question and sets it down in a confirmation of engagement. A concrete question about one application usually fits scoped advice. Want to know first whether your organisation is broadly ready? In the analysis, I speak to the people who do the work, the process owner, IT, and privacy or compliance where relevant. I look at the data, at any vendor proposals, and at what's already been tried.

You get a decision document, not a thick report: which use case, which build choice, which conditions for data and privacy, what change is needed, and what the delivery partner needs to deliver to succeed. All the frameworks and analysis are then yours to keep. Want to carry on yourself? The [AI implementation step-by-step plan](/ai-implementation/step-by-step-plan) helps with the phases that follow.

<p class="scenario-label">Example scenario</p>
<p>A building services firm with 60 staff has a proposal on the table for an AI scheduling module: £42,000 a year, on a three-year contract. The managing director wants to know whether this is the right first step before signing.</p>
<p>Over four days of advisory work, the adviser speaks to the schedulers, the service manager and IT, and holds the proposal up against the firm's own data. The proposal assumes job tickets with a reliable duration and location. In practice, that information is missing from 35 percent of job tickets.</p>
<p>The advice: don't sign yet. First spend three months getting job-ticket records in order, and in the meantime start a smaller application using the AI assistant the firm already has: summarising job sheets for the back office. The decision document sets out the requirements for a later selection, including a test on the firm's own data and an exit clause. The £126,000 three-year commitment stays off the table for now.</p>
