## AI examples by department

The AI examples on this page are deliberately generic: "a wholesaler could..." rather than an invented success story. What they have in common is that the AI does a bounded task inside an existing process. None of them are systems that decide on their own; they're tools that read, sort, summarise or draft a first version.

Those four verbs come up in almost every example. **Reading**: pulling data out of an email, PDF or form. **Sorting**: placing something into a category or giving it a priority. **Summarising**: condensing a long file, conversation or document down to the essentials. **Drafting**: producing a first version a human finishes off. Recognise one of those four in a task that comes up often in your organisation, and you've found a candidate.

This page is a broad list of examples. Which use cases actually deliver results in practice, and which ones almost always stall, is covered in [which AI use cases deliver results](/insights/which-ai-use-cases-deliver-results).

### Customer service

1. **Sorting and routing enquiries.** An online retailer can have every incoming email read and classified by subject (order, return, complaint) and urgency. The email reaches the right team immediately, instead of sitting in a general inbox first.
2. **Draft replies from the knowledge base.** A housing association can have a draft reply prepared for frequently asked questions, based on its own policy documents. The staff member checks, adjusts and sends.
3. **Summarising calls.** An insurer can have a summary and the agreed next steps logged automatically in the customer file after a phone call, so a colleague who calls later knows straight away what's going on.

### Sales

4. **Draft quotes.** A wholesaler can have the products, quantities and delivery preferences pulled from an email enquiry and a draft quote prepared against the price list. The salesperson checks and sends it.
5. **Enriching leads.** A software company can have new enquiries supplemented with public company information and a first estimate of fit. The salesperson decides who to call first.
6. **Preparing for a customer visit.** An account manager at an installation firm can have a summary prepared before a visit, covering order history, open tickets and recent correspondence.

### Marketing

7. **Writing product copy.** An online retailer with thousands of items can have draft copy written from product specifications, in-house style. An editor signs off; prices and sizes come from the product system.
8. **Repurposing content.** A consultancy can have a webinar or presentation turned into a draft article, a newsletter and a few social posts, which the author then sharpens.
9. **Analysing customer feedback.** A hotel chain can have hundreds of reviews sorted into themes (cleanliness, breakfast, staff) and spot which complaints are rising.

### Finance

10. **Reading and matching invoices.** A wholesaler can have incoming invoices read and compared against the purchase order. Only discrepancies reach accounts payable.
11. **Commentary on reports.** A controller can have the narrative commentary for a monthly report drafted from the figures and the previous month. The figures themselves come from the financial system, not the language model.
12. **Preparing payment reminders.** A wholesaler or service provider can have reminders drafted that take payment history and prior agreements with the customer into account, for sign-off by the credit controller.

### HR

13. **Answering questions about policies.** An organisation with an extensive staff handbook can let employees ask an internal assistant about leave, expenses or working from home, with answers that reference the source.
14. **Drafting job adverts.** A recruiter can have a draft job advert written from the role profile. Note: AI that filters applications or assesses candidates is on the AI Act's list of high-risk applications ([Annex III](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/annex-3)). That's a different kind of application from drafting text.
15. **Onboarding and internal knowledge.** A new employee can search policy documents, work instructions and past project files with an assistant. A related application is using AI to capture meeting notes and action points automatically.

## AI examples by sector

Every sector has its own rules and sensitivities. The examples below therefore stay on the preparatory side of the work.

16. **Healthcare.** A healthcare provider can have a draft note prepared from written or dictated input, which the clinician checks and records. The goal is less admin time, not a medical judgement.
17. **Financial services.** A financial institution can have data extracted from statements, identity documents and annual accounts during customer onboarding, while an analyst keeps the judgement call. That pattern is central to document analysis for know-your-customer checks. Note: AI that assesses a person's creditworthiness falls under the AI Act's high-risk applications. More on this in [AI in fintech](/markets/fintech).
18. **Automotive.** A garage or bodyshop can have a customer's description and a mechanic's findings turned into a draft work order or a customer-friendly explanation of the repair. See [AI in automotive](/markets/automotive).
19. **Education.** A teacher can have course material adapted to different levels, or draft practice questions prepared. Assessing learning outcomes or admissions with AI falls under the AI Act's high-risk applications.
20. **Aviation.** A maintenance organisation can let technicians search extensive manuals and procedures with an assistant that always points to the source passage. The technician stays responsible and works to the approved documentation.
21. **Government.** A local authority can have incoming reports and letters categorised and routed to the right department, so residents reach the right handler faster.
22. **Logistics.** A haulage firm can have waybills and delivery notes read and automatically flag discrepancies between ordered and delivered quantities.

What stands out: in heavily regulated sectors, the AI is almost always on the preparatory side of the work. That's no coincidence. The moment an application makes a decision about a person itself, or replaces a professional judgement, heavier requirements kick in under GDPR, the AI Act and sector-specific rules. Letting the AI prepare and the professional decide keeps an application easier to explain and faster to roll out.

Many of these examples are achievable for smaller organisations too, provided you start small. How to approach that as an SME is covered in [AI for small business](/ai-for-business/sme).

## AI examples from everyday life

Many AI applications are already part of daily life without you noticing. Three examples the vendor itself has described:

23. **Spam filters.** Gmail uses machine learning alongside rules to catch spam, phishing and malware, according to Google ([Google Workspace Blog](https://workspace.google.com/blog/product-announcements/ridding-gmail-of-100-million-more-spam-messages-with-tensorflow)).
24. **Predicting travel time.** Google Maps predicts arrival times partly using models DeepMind developed with the Maps team based on live and historical traffic data, as DeepMind described in 2020 ([Google DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/blog/traffic-prediction-with-advanced-graph-neural-networks/)).
25. **Translation.** Google Translate moved to neural machine translation in 2016, translating whole sentences at once rather than piece by piece ([Google](https://blog.google/products/translate/found-translation-more-accurate-fluent-sentences-google-translate/)).

The principle is the same as in the business examples: a model makes an estimate based on patterns, and that estimate is usually right but not always. The difference is that in a business process, you have to arrange yourself who catches the mistakes.

## How do you choose the right application?

A long list of AI possibilities only helps once you pick a few. Four questions make the difference:

- **How often does the task come up?** A small time saving a thousand times a month beats a big insight once a year.
- **How predictable is the input?** The more similar the cases, the more reliable the AI.
- **What does a mistake cost, and who sees it?** A draft a staff member checks is different from a message that goes straight to a customer.
- **Who benefits from it?** Without an owner in the business line, an application stays an experiment.

Three pitfalls come up again and again when choosing:

- **Choosing on impression.** The application that impresses most in a demo is rarely the one that saves the most time. Boring and frequent beats spectacular and rare almost every time.
- **Automating a process that could disappear.** Sometimes a manual step only exists because two systems don't talk to each other. Fix that instead of speeding up the manual work.
- **Forgetting the check step.** If nobody currently looks at the outcome, you need to add that step. That costs time and makes the saving smaller than the first calculation suggested.

Start small after that: one application, one team, real data and a baseline measurement of how long the work currently takes. Let the new way of working run alongside the old one for a few weeks and count how often a staff member has to correct the AI. Only once that's consistently low do you expand to another team or application.

<p>A technical wholesaler with 85 staff picks eleven candidates from a list like this one, spread across five departments. Each candidate gets a score for volume, predictability of the input, cost of a mistake and an owner in the business line.</p>
