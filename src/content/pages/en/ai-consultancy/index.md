## What is AI consulting?

AI consulting is advice on where AI adds value in your organisation, what that takes, and in what order you tackle it. It's less about technology than the name suggests, and mostly about choices: which use cases first, build or buy, which vendor, who becomes the owner, and how you keep it manageable.

The AI consulting label covers everything from strategic advice to agencies that build models and resell licences. I deliberately sit on the advisory side. I help you decide and guide delivery from your side of the table, but I don't build or sell software, AI agents or implementations myself. That means I have no stake in a bigger scope or in a particular vendor. For a closer look at what that work involves day to day, and how it differs from a developer's role, see the [services overview](/services).

What you have in hand at the end are decisions, backed by documents you can reuse yourself: a readiness picture, a prioritised portfolio of use cases, a strategy and roadmap, a selection recommendation, or a governance framework. A full overview of the engagements is on the [services](/services) page.

## When do you need AI consulting?

Not always. A small, reversible choice, such as a tool for one team that you can cancel monthly, is usually better tried than advised on. Outside help pays off when a decision is expensive, hard to reverse, or when there's no shared internal view. Three situations tend to be recognisable.

### You want to start with AI but lack direction

There's no shortage of ideas, and often colleagues already working with AI tools on their own initiative. What's missing is a shared yardstick for deciding what comes first. Without it, the idea that wins is whichever one is lobbied hardest, or whichever vendor gave the best demo. The first step is then a sober view of where you stand, followed by a short list of priorities the leadership team stands behind.

### Pilots aren't delivering results

The pilot worked technically, everyone was pleased, and a year later nothing has scaled. That's rarely down to the technology. The usual causes: the pilot answered the wrong question, ran alongside the real work rather than inside it, had no owner for the next phase, and no agreement on upkeep. In a consulting engagement I make those points explicit before any more budget goes into a pilot.

### You need to comply with the AI Act

The European AI Act is being phased in. According to the [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), the prohibited AI practices have applied since 2 February 2025, the rules for general-purpose AI models since 2 August 2025, and the transparency rules since 2 August 2026. Under the Digital Omnibus, which took effect on 27 July 2026, the obligations for high-risk systems have been postponed: to 2 December 2027 for applications in areas including employment, education, biometrics and critical infrastructure, and to 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products (as of September 2026).

A postponement doesn't mean you can wait. You need to know which AI applications you use or buy and which risk category they fall into, and that takes time. That's why the AI Readiness Assessment includes a risk and compliance scan, and the AI Strategy Sprint includes a risk classification. That's a working tool to structure the conversation, not a legal judgement; involve your lawyer or privacy officer where needed. What the law asks of you is a question of AI policy, covered separately.

### AI Readiness Assessment

The diagnosis. I review your organisation across five dimensions: strategy, data, technology, people and governance, through document analysis, a data scan and interviews across the business, from the business side and IT through to risk and the front line. You get a readiness score per dimension, a gap analysis with the blockers that actually matter, a validated longlist of use cases, a risk and compliance scan, and a 90-day plan with owners. The [AI Readiness Assessment](/services/ai-readiness-assessment) takes {{timing:ai-readiness-assessment}} and is the logical starting point for most organisations.

### AI strategy

Prioritising and deciding. Every candidate use case is assessed the same way: value, feasibility, data readiness, change load, and whether there's an owner in the business who genuinely benefits. The list gets shorter, not longer. A reasoned choice follows per theme between building it yourself, buying, or partnering, including an order-of-magnitude cost over three years. Strategy and roadmap, including this build-buy-partner decision, are covered together on the [AI strategy](/ai-consulting/ai-strategy) page.

### Implementation support

Ship and govern. This is the phase where most engagements stall. The build is done by your own team or by a partner you contract directly. I stay on your side of the table: I stress-test business cases before anything is signed, run vendor conversations in your interest, set up a measurement baseline, and put governance and upkeep in place. If you need someone for this on an ongoing basis, I do that as a [Fractional AI Lead](/services/fractional-ai-lead), a few fixed days a month.

If more hands are needed, I suggest parties I know. How that works, and why I charge no margin on their rate, is covered on the [partner network](/partner-network) page.

## Services within AI consulting

AI consulting covers a few distinct types of question.

### AI strategy and roadmap

For organisations that want to move from scattered ideas to focused choices, and from choices to an executable plan. A strategy sets out your goals, the conditions around data, people and governance, and the use cases that contribute most to them; the roadmap turns that into an owned, budgeted sequence. Read how to [develop an AI strategy and roadmap](/ai-consulting/ai-strategy) that doesn't end up in a drawer.

### AI advice

Not every question needs a full engagement. Sometimes you want a second opinion on a vendor proposal, a sanity check on a business case, or an assessment of an AI company you're considering acquiring. For the latter there's [AI Due Diligence](/services/ai-due-diligence); other scoped advisory questions and second opinions get a fixed price per assignment, the same way.

### Hiring an AI consultant

Hiring an AI consultant is a bet on one person, so that person needs to be right. It's worth it in a few recognisable situations: the decision is expensive and hard to reverse, there's an internal stalemate between departments with no neutral tie-breaker, you need to defend an investment to the board, you're doing this for the first time under time pressure, or initiatives are running with no one keeping an eye on the overall AI agenda. A small, reversible choice, by contrast, is usually better tried than advised on.

A freelance AI consultant is typically flexible and quick to start, and you work directly with the person you spoke to first, with no layers in between. An agency or large consultancy brings more capacity, specialist teams and institutional cover, which matters for large transformations, heavy delivery work, or engagements where auditability leads. The trade-off with a freelancer is limited scale and dependence on one person.

Whichever you choose, the same three things separate the good candidates from the rest:

- **Demonstrable experience with your type of question**, not just with AI in general. Ask what comparable assignments looked like, what the decision was, and whether you can pick your own reference rather than only the ones offered.
- **Independence.** Some AI advisers are also an implementation partner or a platform reseller. That's not necessarily wrong, but you should know it. Ask explicitly whether they receive fees, discounts or partner status from vendors who might come into play, and ask for that in writing. My own answer: I don't build or sell software, take no commissions, and charge no margin on partners; any existing vendor relationship gets disclosed before that party comes into the picture.
- **Clear deliverables.** Ask upfront what you'll have in hand at the end and when the assignment is considered done. Good deliverables are made to decide with: a prioritised list of use cases with owners, an AI strategy with explicit choices, an AI roadmap with planning and budget, or a decision document for a vendor choice.

On cost: I work with fixed packages and a fixed price or fixed monthly fee agreed upfront, rather than an open hourly rate, across three hiring formats: scoped advice at a fixed price per assignment, a fixed-price engagement such as the AI Readiness Assessment or AI Strategy Sprint, or an ongoing part-time role as [Fractional AI Lead](/services/fractional-ai-lead). ## AI consulting for larger organisations

In larger organisations, the problem is rarely a shortage of AI initiatives. It's the lack of coherence between them.

### Bringing fragmented AI initiatives together

Every department or site has set something up, each with its own vendor and its own definition of success. I start with an honest inventory: what's actually running, what it costs, and who uses it. All initiatives then move under one decision framework and one set of metrics. Some continue, some are deliberately stopped. The latter causes discussion, but it also frees up capacity.

### Working with IT and internal teams

I work alongside your IT department, data team and architects, not around them. They know the systems; I bring the decision frameworks, the comparison between options, and the translation into business terms. Whether you build it yourself, buy it, or bring in a partner is a decision you make together, with the costs and dependencies of the following years factored in.

### Governance and compliance

Governance should make the work better, not slower. I keep it as light as possible: a register of AI applications, a gate before anything goes into production, a periodic review and an escalation path. For leadership teams and boards that want to fulfil their oversight role on AI, there's the [Executive Briefing](/services/executive-briefing): {{timing:executive-briefing}}, prepared for your sector.

## AI consulting for small and medium-sized businesses

In SMEs the questions are often the same, but the scale is different. There's usually no dedicated AI team, the budget is limited, and the owner or managing director decides directly. That makes things simpler in several ways.

### Practical and within budget

You don't need a months-long engagement. An AI Readiness Assessment can also be scoped to one department or one process area, which is quicker and costs less. A defined question or a second opinion gets a fixed price per assignment. And the outcome can also be that you shouldn't buy anything yet. More on the choices that matter for smaller businesses is covered elsewhere on this site.

### Fast first results

Quick results usually come not from the most spectacular application, but from a dull task that takes a lot of time and whose data already sits in one system. The 90-day plan from the assessment names what you'll tackle in the first three months, with owners and decision points. Ideally that includes an application that can start straight away, with an owner and a metric. Whether that result sticks depends mostly on how well the application fits daily work.

## Data and AI consulting

AI is only as good as the data it runs on. Even so, "get the data in order first" is just as often an excuse as a genuine necessity. A complete data landscape is never finished; the question you can actually answer is whether the data is good enough for one specific application.

In data and AI consulting, I therefore look at four things per use case: clear definitions, provenance and traceability, quality and completeness, and accessibility. Definitions are usually the biggest and most underestimated problem; infrastructure the least urgent. You build the foundation alongside the first applications, not ahead of them.

Machine learning consultants and data scientists focus on building and training models. My role sits one step earlier and one step above: deciding which model you need, whether to build it yourself, and how you manage upkeep and risk.
