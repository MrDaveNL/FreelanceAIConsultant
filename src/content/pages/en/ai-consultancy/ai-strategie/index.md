## What is an AI strategy?

An AI strategy is the set of choices that determines what your organisation uses AI for, why, and what needs to change to make that happen. A good AI strategy answers four questions: which business outcomes you want to achieve, which applications contribute most to them, what that requires in terms of data, people and governance, and what you deliberately choose not to do.

That last point is often forgotten. A strategy that keeps every option open helps no one decide. The value sits precisely in the short list, and in the reasoned list of what didn't make the cut.

An AI vision is something different. A vision describes a direction ("we want to handle customer questions faster and better"); the strategy turns that into choices you can measure and steer by. The difference between an AI strategy that sits on a shelf and one that works usually comes down to that translation.

## Why you need an AI strategy

Without a strategy, AI use still happens, just uncoordinated. Teams buy tools, departments start pilots, vendors turn up with demos. Each initiative can be sensible on its own, but together they deliver little. There's no shared yardstick for deciding what gets budget, so whoever lobbies hardest wins.

An AI strategy solves three practical problems:

- **Priority:** you can explain why one initiative gets budget and another doesn't.
- **Coherence:** applications share the same data, the same upkeep arrangements and the same risk check, instead of every team reinventing the wheel.
- **Accountability:** leadership, the board and regulators get a rationale they can steer by, including the risks.

A strategy doesn't need to be large. For a mid-sized organisation, a few pages with goals, a prioritised portfolio and clear conditions are often enough. How a strategy fits within the broader work of [AI consulting](/ai-consulting) is covered on the overview page.

## Parts of a good AI strategy

A usable AI strategy has four parts. Miss one, and delivery runs into it sooner or later.

### Goals and business case

Start from the outcome, not the technology. "Use generative AI for customer contact" is a direction. "Lower the handling time for first-line questions without customer satisfaction dropping" is a goal you can steer by. Each goal needs an owner in the business, an initial value estimate with visible assumptions, and a metric.

The business case doesn't need to be precise to the pound at this stage, but it does need to be honest. Time saved is only a saving once someone actually spends that time differently.

### Data and infrastructure

A data strategy for AI doesn't need to start with a complete data landscape. The question isn't "is our data in order?" but "is this data good enough for this decision?". Definitions usually matter more than volume or infrastructure: four systems with three definitions of "customer" is a bigger problem than a missing data warehouse.

You build the foundation alongside the first use cases, not ahead of them.

### People and skills

AI changes how people work. A strategy should therefore name who will work differently, which skills that requires, and who manages the applications once delivered. Think of owners in the business, a point of contact for AI, and training tied to specific tasks rather than general explanations of tools.

This is also where the question sits of whether you need your own AI lead, and if so, when. For many organisations that isn't a full-time role at the start.

### Governance and risk

Governance determines who can decide, how an application reaches production, and what happens if something goes wrong. Keep it light: a register of applications, a gate before production, a periodic review and an escalation path. Risk covers privacy, dependence on vendors, and the obligations under the AI Act.

## From strategy to roadmap

A strategy only becomes real once someone turns it into a quarterly goal. Most strategies stall not on the content, but on three missing translations: turning ambition into measurable outcomes, assigning one owner with budget and a mandate per use case, and measuring at the level where the value was promised.

The roadmap is where that translation comes together: which use cases come first, what conditions they need, what they cost, and when you decide whether to continue or stop. A good roadmap is short, not a list of twenty initiatives everyone is enthusiastic about and no one owns, but a handful of use cases each with a name attached. Alongside it sits a list of what deliberately doesn't happen, and why; that rejected list stops declined ideas quietly coming back.

A roadmap is not a project plan either. It works in horizons: the first three months set out concretely, the period after that in broader blocks. At fixed points you check whether the assumptions still hold.

Start with what already exists: which AI applications and pilots are running, what do they cost, and who actually uses them? Some of what's running on paper may already have quietly stopped in practice. Alongside that, capture the starting position of data, systems, skills and governance; you'll need that baseline later to say whether the roadmap delivered anything. The AI Readiness Assessment covers this step and, with its 90-day plan, delivers a first version of the roadmap.

Next, assess every candidate the same way: which business outcome, how much value, how feasible, how ready is the data, how large is the change load, and is there an owner in the business who genuinely benefits? Score this together with the people who know the processes, not alone in a spreadsheet.

Per selected use case, settle what needs to be true before it can start: which data sources and definitions need to be in order, who will work differently, who manages the application once delivered, and which risk check is needed, including under the AI Act. This is also where the build, buy or partner choice sits; it shapes your costs and dependencies for years, so make that choice per theme rather than per tool.

Finally, place the use cases in horizons. Horizon 1, the first three months, is planned concretely, with owners and a start date. Horizons 2 and 3 are described in broader blocks. Many roadmaps combine quick wins with a few medium-term investments, so you learn early while the foundation grows alongside it. Give each use case an order-of-magnitude budget, including upkeep after delivery, which is easily forgotten even though an application without upkeep can quietly perform worse over time. Set a decision point per use case with an agreed criterion for continuing or stopping.

### What a roadmap delivers

- **Prioritised portfolio:** the use cases going ahead, with scoring and reasoning per use case.
- **Rejected list:** what deliberately doesn't happen, with the reason attached.
- **Horizon plan:** what happens in which period, with dependencies between use cases visible.
- **Owner per use case:** one name, in the business, with budget and the mandate to stop.
- **Conditions:** the data, people, governance and build-buy-partner choice needed per use case.
- **Order-of-magnitude budget:** per use case, including upkeep after delivery.
- **Decision points and metrics:** when you decide to continue or stop, and on what basis.
- **Governance of the roadmap itself:** who owns the whole, and how often it gets revisited.

### AI strategy and roadmap for smaller organisations

The same four roadmap steps apply in a smaller organisation, just at a smaller scale. There's usually no dedicated AI team, budget is limited, and the lines are short, which makes a few things simpler: the owner or managing director is often the roadmap owner directly, and the core group can be two or three people.

A few points worth watching for smaller organisations:

- **Limit yourself to two or three use cases** in the first horizon; more is hard to guide properly without dedicated AI capacity.
- **Choose applications that fit existing software first** and run on a single data source, so you don't need anything built just to learn.
- **Plan upkeep explicitly:** who checks that it still works if there's no IT department?
- **Factor in data protection** as soon as personal data enters an application.
