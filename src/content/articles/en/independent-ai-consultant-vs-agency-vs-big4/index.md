<h2>When do you choose which?</h2>
<p>AI projects differ sharply in purpose, complexity and context. The choice between an independent consultant, an agency or a Big 4 firm has a direct impact on speed, agility, cost and outcome. No single option wins on every measure, and anyone who claims otherwise is selling you something.</p>
<p>I'm the independent consultant in this line-up myself. That doesn't make me objective, so read this piece with that in mind. What I can do is be honest about the cases where you're better off not calling me. They're all in here.</p>

<h2>2. What does this mean in practice?</h2>
<div class="grid-3 option-grid">
<div class="tone-yellow"><h3>Independent AI consultant</h3><p>You work directly with a senior expert who adapts quickly and does only what's genuinely needed. You get one opinion, not a negotiated position.</p></div>
<div class="tone-aqua"><h3>Agency</h3><p>You bring in specialist capacity and build methodically. Ideal for bespoke work and scalable delivery, especially if you don't have your own engineering team.</p></div>
<div class="tone-salmon"><h3>Big 4</h3><p>You choose assurance, governance and worldwide coverage. Suited to large transformations, mergers and projects where compliance leads.</p></div>
</div>

<h3>Where the independent consultant wins</h3>
<p>On focused, decision-driven questions. A strategy sprint, a vendor selection, a second opinion on a business case, a fractional leadership role. Work where one experienced head is worth more than ten pairs of hands. The lead time is shorter because there's no internal alignment needed between consultants, and the senior you spoke to at the introductory call is the same person who does the work.</p>

<h3>Where the independent consultant loses</h3>
<p>As soon as volume is needed. An implementation with eight developers, a migration across fifteen countries, a programme that runs for two years: I can't deliver that, and shouldn't try to. On projects sensitive to a single point of failure, a one-person practice is also a real risk: if I'm out for three months, you have a problem. An agency or Big 4 firm doesn't have that problem.</p>

<h3>Where agencies are underestimated</h3>
<p>Agencies sometimes get accused of mainly wanting to build. That's fair: it's their business model. But if you already know what you want to build, that's exactly what you need. A good agency brings engineering discipline, reusable components, and a team that has taken this type of system into production more than once. Don't underestimate how much that's worth.</p>

<h3>Where the Big 4 are underestimated</h3>
<p>The criticism of large consultancies is well known: expensive, lots of junior staff, thick reports. That happens. But on projects where the board carries liability, where regulators are watching, or where a decision has to clear an audit committee, you're buying something an independent consultant can't deliver: institutional cover and a methodology that's audit-proof. That's not overhead, that's the product.</p>

<blockquote>Start small, learn fast and scale with focus. That's how you make AI workable and valuable.</blockquote>

<h2>3. The conflict-of-interest conversation</h2>
<p>With AI, this goes further than with ordinary advisory work, because many advisers are also implementation partners for specific platforms. That isn't automatically wrong. A firm that builds a platform every day knows more about it than someone assessing it from a distance. But you need to know whether that's the case.</p>
<p>Ask every party these four questions, regardless of size:</p>
<ul>
<li>Do you receive any fee, discount or partner status from suppliers who might be recommended in this project?</li>
<li>Do you have an implementation practice on the platforms that could end up on the shortlist?</li>
<li>What's your revenue split between implementation and advisory work?</li>
<li>Is the consultant who writes the advice rewarded on follow-on revenue?</li>
</ul>
<p>Honest answers to these questions matter more than the answer itself. A firm that says "we're an implementation partner for three platforms, and here's how we account for that" is more trustworthy than one that claims to be "fully independent" without backing it up.</p>

<h2>4. How do you make the right choice?</h2>
<p>Answer these questions and you'll know faster which option fits best:</p>
<ul>
<li>What's the primary goal: making a decision, building something, or demonstrably being in control of something?</li>
<li>How urgent is the lead time, and what does delay cost?</li>
<li>What level of governance and compliance is required, and who's watching?</li>
<li>Do you have in-house capability to build alongside and maintain it afterwards?</li>
<li>What's the available budget, and how much flexibility do you need in it?</li>
<li>What happens if the chosen party drops out halfway through?</li>
</ul>

<h2>5. The combination that often works best</h2>
<p>In practice, I rarely see a clean choice. The setup that works out best most often: an independent senior handles the strategy and the selection, and an agency or systems integrator does the build, with the adviser on the client's side of the table during the build. The adviser then has no stake in the implementation's scope growing, which removes exactly the tension that otherwise shows up around month four.</p>
<p>It costs you a bit more in advisory hours. It usually saves you a multiple of that in extra work.</p>
