<h2>Where AI delivers the most value in customer service</h2>
<p>What I often see: companies want to start with a customer-facing chatbot, while the fastest win sits internally. Have a model sort emails first and draft replies that a staff member checks. That way you learn what the model gets right and wrong, without a customer ever seeing the mistakes.</p>
<p>That order isn't a detour, it's the fastest route to a chatbot that actually works. A staff member correcting a draft reply instead of typing one from scratch works faster, and at the same time you build up exactly what does and doesn't hold up for your customer service, before you put that risk in front of a customer.</p>

<h2>Chatbot, email triage or supporting staff</h2>
<p>These are three different applications that each need a different level of maturity, not three steps in the same funnel:</p>
<ul>
  <li><b>Email triage.</b> The model sorts incoming questions by topic and urgency, and drafts a reply that a staff member checks before it's sent. Lowest risk, fastest start, and exactly the place to learn what the model can and can't handle.</li>
  <li><b>Supporting staff.</b> An assistant that searches the knowledge base alongside a conversation or ticket and suggests an answer, while the staff member runs the conversation. The customer notices nothing of the AI, but the staff member works faster and more consistently.</li>
  <li><b>Customer-facing chatbot.</b> The model talks to the customer directly, without a staff member checking first. Highest risk, because mistakes are immediately visible to the customer, and it places the highest demands on the knowledge base and the handoff to a human.</li>
</ul>
<p>Most organisations can take on the first two within a few weeks. A customer-facing chatbot only becomes sensible once those first two have shown you which questions the model can handle and which it can't.</p>

<h2>What an AI chatbot for customer service needs</h2>
<p>A chatbot is only as good as the source it answers from, not the model behind it. Without a clean, up-to-date knowledge base, a chatbot simply repeats the confusion already sitting in your documentation, just stated with more confidence. Three conditions are the minimum:</p>
<ul>
  <li><b>A knowledge base that's accurate.</b> Outdated or contradictory answers in your help centre or internal wiki come straight back in every answer the chatbot gives.</li>
  <li><b>Clear boundaries.</b> What the chatbot is allowed to answer itself, and what always goes to a human, such as complaints, billing disputes or anything with legal weight.</li>
  <li><b>A smooth handoff.</b> The customer needs to reach a staff member without hassle and without repeating their question the moment the chatbot gets stuck.</li>
</ul>
<p>How to set up that kind of knowledge base and the automation behind it is covered in more detail under <a href="/ai-automation/ai-workflows">AI workflows</a>. More broadly, for processes beyond customer service, you'll find applications and examples under <a href="/knowledge-base/ai-examples">AI examples</a> and in <a href="/insights/which-ai-use-cases-deliver-results">which AI use cases deliver results</a>.</p>

<h2>Costs and payback time (indicative)</h2>
<p>What this costs doesn't primarily depend on the licence, but on three things: how many questions you handle per month, how deep the integration with your existing systems needs to be, and how much staff review time the start-up phase takes. Email triage with a standard tool is cheaper and pays back faster than a chatbot that talks to customers directly and needs to be connected to your CRM and knowledge base.</p>

<h2>The most common mistakes</h2>
<ul>
  <li><b>Starting straight away with the customer-facing chatbot.</b> The highest-risk application as a first step, without having learned yet what the model can and can't handle.</li>
  <li><b>No smooth handoff to a human.</b> A chatbot that leaves a customer stuck with no way out costs more trust than it ever saves in time.</li>
  <li><b>A knowledge base that hasn't been cleaned up.</b> Contradictory or outdated content produces contradictory or outdated answers, just phrased more convincingly now.</li>
  <li><b>Not disclosing that it's AI.</b> Besides the trust risk, for a chatbot this is also a legal obligation; see below.</li>
  <li><b>Nobody measures what's going wrong.</b> Without spot-checking handled conversations, you only notice a quality problem once customers start complaining about it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Transparency obligation: disclose that it's AI</h2>
<p>Under the EU AI Act, the transparency obligation in Article 50 has applied since 2 August 2026: a chatbot must make it clear to a user that they are dealing with an AI system, unless that's already obvious from the context. For customer service, that means in practice a short, visible notice at the start of the conversation, not fine print at the bottom of the page.</p>
<p>This obligation is separate from what happened to the AI Act's high-risk rules; the background on that, and what was and wasn't delayed, is covered in the article on the Digital Omnibus. The basic explanation of the AI Act and the risk categories is covered in <a href="/ai-policy/ai-act">the EU AI Act for businesses</a>.</p>

<h2>Step-by-step plan</h2>
<ol>
  <li><b>Start with email triage.</b> Have the model sort and draft, with a staff member checking. This also doubles as your learning phase for the next steps.</li>
  <li><b>Clean up the knowledge base.</b> Remove contradictory or outdated content before feeding it to a model, in any form.</li>
  <li><b>Add support for staff.</b> Have the model search alongside the conversation and suggest an answer, while the staff member runs it.</li>
  <li><b>Set the boundaries for a customer-facing chatbot.</b> Which questions can it answer itself, and when does the conversation always go to a human?</li>
  <li><b>Put the transparency notice and the handoff in place</b> before the chatbot goes live, not after.</li>
  <li><b>Measure and adjust.</b> Spot-check conversations, and set a fixed moment to keep the knowledge base current.</li>
</ol>
